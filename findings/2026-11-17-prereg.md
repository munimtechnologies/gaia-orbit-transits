# Pre-registration: Gaia DR3 astrometric orbits x TESS, first pass (job gaia-orbit-tess-v1)

Written on 2026-11-17, before any Gaia query or TESS download for this project was run.
By MV2 of Munim, Inc.; made with AI agents. CC BY 4.0.

## Question

Do near-edge-on Gaia DR3 astrometric orbits predict eclipses or transits at the times
TESS observed? If they do, can this find new long-period eclipsing or transiting
companions that have astrometric masses?

## Fixed settings

G < 14.5, |cos i| < 0.2, 600 Monte Carlo draws per orbit, eclipse probability >= 0.002.
Substellar-class orbits (dark-companion mass < 0.075 Msun) go first, then the rest in
order of eclipse probability, up to 400 targets and a 200-minute budget. TESS search:
SNR >= 7 and durations of 2-24 h. A "match" is window-match BF > 10. A "strong match"
also needs SNR > 10, an event away from segment edges, and no event at the same time
in two or more other targets.

## Predictions

1. **Method check.** Some targets are also in Gaia's own eclipsing-binary table
   (`vari_eclipsing_binary`). Of those with a predicted conjunction covered by TESS
   (coverage > 0.5), at least half will show a match (BF > 3). If fewer do, the
   conjunction timing (ω, Tp, or the Gaia time reference) is wrong and no other
   result is trusted.
2. **Calibration.** The number of strong matches will be within a factor of 3 of
   the job's own `expected_eclipses_covered`. Far fewer would mean the eclipse
   probabilities are overestimated, most likely because the catalogue correlations
   are ignored.
3. **Mostly known stars.** Most strong matches (> 50%) will be stellar eclipsing
   binaries already listed in VSX, SIMBAD or the Gaia EB table.
4. **Substellar.** 0 to 2 substellar-class targets will have strong matches. Any that
   do will more likely be luminous near-twin binaries (eclipse depth > 5% and a flat
   or V-shaped bottom) than a true brown-dwarf or planet transit, whose depth is
   about (R2/R1)^2.

## What would be "wow"

A transit by a brown dwarf or giant planet on a long-period (> 30 d) orbit that Gaia
astrometry predicted. That would give a companion with both a true mass and a radius,
a rare benchmark found from astrometry first.
